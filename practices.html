Our Practices

How a collective actually operates is where values either take hold or quietly evaporate. This page describes the specific practices through which the Careoperative tries to live what it believes.

Guiding principles

Our Code of Collaboration opens with five articles that describe how we aspire to treat each other and the work. These are not aspirational slogans — they are behavioral commitments, named so that we can hold each other to account.

Respect colleagues' needs, time, opinions, and limits — and your own. Listen without interrupting. Acknowledge that differences of discipline, background, and circumstance are not always visible. When critique is offered, couple it with a constructive follow-up.

Follow through on commitments. Be ambitious, but don't promise what cannot be delivered. If you're struggling with a task, pass it on rather than let it stall. Come prepared. Be pragmatic: deliberate but not slow to decide.

Collaborate vocally to avoid misunderstandings. Let people know early if something changes. Share what others need to know, without flooding the channels. Everyone is busy; good communication is a form of care.

Cooperate by making use of your strengths and letting others apply theirs. When a process stops working, name it, take time to reflect, and bring suggestions rather than complaints. Cover each other's gaps; use them as learning opportunities.

Treat each other fairly and justly while respecting our differences. We do not tolerate discrimination, harassment, or bullying. We recognize that our collective formed through a selection process we did not design, which may not have been fully equitable — and we seek to remedy that through ongoing attention to inclusivity.

Membership and participation

Not everyone can be equally present all the time — and pretending otherwise would be dishonest about how academic life actually works. At our annual meeting, members choose their level of participation for the year ahead. Four tiers are recognised, each with corresponding expectations.

The Caretakers — elected annually — hold the stewardship role for the collective: coordinating logistics, holding the calendar, and ensuring continuity when the group's energy is dispersed. The role rotates so that the skills and responsibilities it develops are shared across the group over time.

Decision-making

Our decision-making is inspired by sociocratic principles. The aim is not to produce perfect agreement but to ensure everyone has a genuine say, and that the group can move without being paralysed by the need for unanimity.

Tap a step to expand its detail.

A person or sub-group summarises the key elements and justification of a proposed decision — its substance, its rationale, and what it is asking the group to commit to.

An open round inviting clarifying questions only. This is not the moment for objections or advocacy; it is for making sure everyone understands the proposal before responding to it.

An amended or counter-proposal is offered if the clarifications reveal that the original needs adjustment. The group can iterate before moving to a decision.

Each participant signals agreement or disagreement — thumbs-up or thumbs-down. This makes visible where the group actually stands, rather than allowing a few voices to create a false impression of consensus.

Disagreeing participants explain their objections. This is where real differences surface — and where the group learns what it needs to address before a durable decision is possible.

The group reaches one of four outcomes: full consensus (agreed); slight disagreement (accepted as good enough for now, safe enough to try — GEFNSETT); substantial disagreement (needs more work, returns to a future agenda); or a clear follow-up with responsibilities distributed.

Where time is short, the group can be asked to endorse a decision as GEFNSETT — good enough for now, safe enough to try. The explicit acknowledgement that a decision is provisional lowers the stakes enough that progress becomes possible. Silence is treated as acquiescence if notice for review has been given and time has elapsed.

Publication and authorship

The Careoperative publishes collectively under the name O. Care — or, in more recent work, Dr. Care — as shared first author, with remaining authors listed alphabetically. The reasoning is described on the About page and in our published work. Here we set out the specific protocols that make this practice workable.

Any member intending to develop a manuscript that draws on the intellectual contributions of other members — including ideas generated in group sessions — is expected to notify all members in writing as early as possible, preferably at the stage of conceptualization. Members then have the equivalent of ten working days to respond: to express interest in being actively included, or to indicate they prefer not to participate.

This notice requirement applies even to work intended as single-authored, where collective intellectual input may be implicated. It prevents the kind of ambiguity about credit and contribution that can quietly damage working relationships.

Opting in means accepting the duties of authorship: substantive contribution to the work, participation in review and revision, and accountability for the published result. A member who has contributed ideas but chooses to opt out of a paper will be acknowledged appropriately in citations, footnotes, or acknowledgements — but will not appear as an author.

We adopt the COPE definition of authorship, which sets a higher bar than mere contribution. This is intentional: we do not pad author lists, and we do not exclude people who have done the work.

Where senior and junior researchers co-author, or where members with institutional leadership roles collaborate with those without them, power differentials can distort the process. Junior researchers may hesitate to question a senior co-author; those in non-leadership positions may fear that honest disagreement carries professional risk.

We name this explicitly in our Code of Collaboration and build in a recourse: in cases where these tensions arise, independent members of the collective or an external publication ethics process can be brought in to support communication and resolution.

Conflict and repair

Disagreements, tensions, and conflicts are not aberrations in collective work — they are inevitable features of it. They reveal real differences. They are also, if handled well, opportunities for emotional learning and for building the kind of assertiveness and mutual understanding that sustains a long-running collaboration.

Prevention through structure

Much of our conflict prevention is structural rather than reactive. We developed a shared vision before we needed to make hard decisions. We established decision-making procedures and identified roles before disputes arose. We elected Caretakers with a mandate for continuity. We created regular processes — quarterly and annual reviews — for examining not just what we are producing but how we are working together.

We also actively worked to avoid competitive dynamics. The authorship model is one expression of this; so is the explicit commitment in our Code to celebrating collectively and creating space for the group to feel its underlying unity across visible differences.

Resolution protocol

When a conflict cannot be resolved through direct conversation, our protocol moves in steps: the parties involved are first supported separately, to create a secure space; then brought together, with the aim of clarifying perceptions, distinguishing positions from underlying interests and needs, and finding whatever partial agreements are available. Written or oral joint agreements are developed where useful.

We also maintain a committee of mediators — members of the collective not directly involved in a dispute — who can be called upon to facilitate this process. The goal is not to suppress conflict but to work through it in a way that leaves the relationship, and the collective, stronger.
